Failure clinicThe reveal: our trained model misses new movies

The same 8 groups as the bets. Each row: trained model minus the best simple feed in that group, with a 95% interval. Then switch to the test set: does it still hold?

One row is far left. Does it still hold on data nobody looked at?

  • Users: the model wins in all four groups, by 0.07–0.08, new users included. The room's cold-start bet loses.
  • Movies: <1 year (n=104): model 0.344 vs for_you 0.783, −0.44. 1–5 years: −0.03, interval just below 0. 15+ years: +0.20; this group brings about 85% of the model's average gain.
  • Test set: the <1 year loss holds (−0.38); the 1–5 year "loss" does not (its interval crosses 0). Lesson: a finding on validation is a hypothesis until the test set confirms it. This chart is panel B of the lab's atlas.
  • If asked what the interval is: the range of results we would get with other moments of the same kind. The lab notebook computes it (bootstrap) and shows how it is done.

Failure clinicWhy does it miss new movies? How we found out

  1. Find the group. The reveal: movies under a year old, 104 moments, −0.44.
  2. Count. In 40 of the 104 moments the liked movie is not in the model's top 10. for_you has it in its top 10 every time.
  3. Look at one moment (right).
  4. Compare all 104. Liked movie vs the model's top 10, on average: freshness 0.90 vs 0.19 · likes so far 0.18 vs 0.53 · age 0.02 vs 0.51.
  5. Test the idea. Give the same 104 movies more likes so far (0.18 → 0.40): the model's average P(like) rises from 0.29 to 0.36.

A failure is useful only with a why. Here is the path from "this group is red" to a sentence we can defend.

  • Steps 1–2 say how big the problem is; step 3 makes it concrete; step 4 finds the signals that differ; step 5 checks the explanation by changing one signal and watching the model react.
  • Step 5 also rules out the simpler story "new is bad": making the same movies older lowers the model's score. The problem is "no history", not "new".
  • This is exactly what Task 4 of the lab asks for in why: name the signals, not just the group.

Theory · challengeWho wins depends on what your users like

The overall score is an average over all likes, and every like counts once. So the most common kind of like decides the winner. On our data most likes go to old movies; on InstaKill they go to new posts. Move the sliders and watch the ranking.

What it means: there is no "best strategy" in general. A score is true only for the users it was measured on.

1 · Test on your users

Evaluate on data like your app's: InstaKill's own likes (evaluate.py).

2 · Report per group

Show the score for each group, not only the average.

3 · Decide with the groups

Different winners? Serve each group its winner, e.g. for_you for new posts.

The four strategies keep exactly the scores we measured in each age group. We only change how many likes fall in each group. Who wins now?

  • Each row on the left says which strategy is best for that kind of like: for_you for movies under a year old, popularity for 1–5 years, the trained model for older movies.
  • Press "InstaKill: mostly new posts" (60% of likes on new items): for_you wins (about 0.56 vs 0.36). Back to "Our data": the trained model wins.
  • The sentence to keep: a ranking of strategies is valid only for the users it was measured on.
  • What to do, in order: (1) measure on data that looks like your app, which is why the InstaKill evaluation runs on your database (trap 2 of the protocol); (2) report every group, not only the average; (3) when groups have different winners, a mixed feed (for example for_you for posts under a year old, learned for the rest) is a decision the per-group numbers can defend. This week students only measure; changing the product comes later.

Theory · gameA clearer chart makes the analysis easier

Six charts from today's data. Find what makes each one misleading, then compare with the clear version: same numbers, easier answer.

The numbers are correct in every chart. Only the drawing makes the analysis harder. What would you change?

  • Chart 1: the axis starts at 0.37, so a 20% gap looks ten times bigger. Takeaway: bars start at 0.
  • Chart 2: no interval, no count. Animation (50 moments) looks like a loss; with intervals it is nothing. Takeaway: interval and n on every comparison.
  • Chart 3: a pie of four separate scores. The shares mean nothing; bars compare the scores directly.
  • Chart 4: the same two strategies swap colours between two charts. Readers carry the colours over and read the opposite.
  • Chart 5: correct numbers, but no question, no axis label, no group names. Nobody can tell what it shows.
  • Chart 6: rainbow colours on a difference that can be negative. The clear version (red below 0, white at 0, blue above) shows the new-movie column at a glance.
  • The lab's chart linter checks titles, labels, intervals, bars from 0 and centred colours.

InstaKillInstaKill: replay your own feed at time T

Freeze your database at T: before T is what the app knew, the likes after T are the answers. Ask your real GET /feed code for each user's top 10. (Invented dots.)

Same question as the lab, on your data, through your code. Do not re-implement the strategies in the script.

  • Blue = history, gold = answers, grey = likes on posts that did not exist at T (ignored).
  • Later T = more history, fewer answers. Default: the moment by which 80% of the likes happened.
  • The leakage test checks the snapshot has nothing at or after T (the W6 lesson). Follows derived from likes are re-derived in the snapshot.
